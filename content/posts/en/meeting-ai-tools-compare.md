Meeting tools differ between "transcription" and "organizing". Even with perfect transcription the minutes can fail, and even a smooth summary is **useless if decisions and owners are missing**. This post lays out the order to check when choosing a tool.

## Decide first: what are you automating

There are three things in a meeting you can automate:

1. **Transcription** — turning speech into text
2. **Speaker separation** — dividing who said what
3. **Organizing** — pulling out decisions, owners and deadlines into a table

Each tool is strong in a different place. Some transcribe best, some summarize best. Decide **the step where we spend the most time** first, and the comparison becomes easy.

## 1. Korean recognition quality

Choosing from an English demo alone fails. Korean meetings split on these points:

- **Proper nouns**: names, company names, product names
- **Numbers**: amounts, dates, ratios (where transcription errors are most dangerous)
- **Specialized terms**: does it know the vocabulary of our industry
- **Overlap**: how well it holds up when people talk at once
- **Dialect and accent**: does it waver in regional or on-site meetings

Test it yourself with five minutes of real meeting audio. Writing down **the list of words it got wrong** at that point makes the comparison far clearer.

## 2. Speaker separation

The core of minutes is "who agreed to do what". Without speaker separation, ownership blurs. Check:

- Are speakers separated automatically
- Is it easy to fix by hand when it is wrong
- Can a person assign speaker names

## 3. Structure of the summary

A good meeting summary is a **table**, not a paragraph.

| Item | Content |
|---|---|
| Decision | what was decided |
| Owner | who does it |
| Deadline | by when |
| Deferred | what was postponed |
| Next meeting | when, and what to look at |

If the tool can pull out exactly this structure, the time you spend fixing it drops sharply. The method is covered step by step in [organizing meeting notes with AI](/en/p/ai-meeting-notes-workflow).

## 4. Security and data boundaries

Meeting audio contains personal remarks, contract terms and HR information. Check before choosing:

- Where the audio file is stored
- Whether it is used for training and can that be turned off
- Can an admin control access
- Are deletion and export possible
- Is there a notice for external attendees

A tool you cannot answer these questions for is not adopted first, however good its features are. See [handling personal data in AI work](/en/p/privacy-in-ai-work).

## 5. Connection to the meeting flow

A tool's usefulness depends on where it attaches — before, during or after the meeting.

- **Before**: can you load the agenda and material in advance
- **During**: does it transcribe live, or only record
- **After**: can the summary go straight to a calendar, document or chat

Without a connection, people bridge with copy and paste, and that repetition erases the effect of automation.

## The comparison experiment

1. Give each tool **the same five minutes of meeting audio**.
2. Count transcription error words.
3. Count speaker separation errors.
4. Count how many decisions, owners and deadlines the summary got right.
5. Record the time it took to fix by hand.
6. Show the summary to a colleague who was not at the meeting and ask whether they understood it.

Step 6 matters most. The purpose of a summary is **that someone who was not there can understand**.

## What to look at in the pricing

- Per minute or per user
- How many minutes the free tier gives per month
- Whether speaker separation and summary are paid-only
- Retention period and overage charges
- Tax, exchange rate and payment method

Prices change often, so check each tool's **official page**.
