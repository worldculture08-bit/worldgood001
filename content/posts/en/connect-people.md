Connecting people and work sounds impressive, but in practice it is a delicate responsibility. Because a single introduction moves someone's time, trust and opportunity. If you forget that a light introduction can produce a heavy result, a network turns into noise quickly.

## Ask before introducing

- Does the other person actually want this connection now?
- Can you briefly explain the context on both sides?
- Have you decided your own exit point after the introduction?
- Were you careful that sensitive details do not stay in a messenger?

A good introduction does not happen because "they'd probably like to know each other." It works when **the two needs meet**. An introduction made without checking need may look like kindness, but on the receiving side it becomes homework.

## What to record

When, who, and for what reason you connected them. And how the outcome unfolded. Not only the connections that went well — the awkward ones teach you the next step too. "An introduction that was declined" is an asset. Was the timing early, was the context missing, did the other person have no room? A short note makes the next introduction less clumsy.

A connection is a process, not an event. Leaving a trace of that process, even short, keeps the network from becoming **a pile of business cards** and keeps it a working relationship. That is not a demand to reduce relationships to work. It only means that if your role connects work and people, the history of those connections is part of the work.
