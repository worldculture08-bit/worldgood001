When the topic comes up, it splits into two extremes. "You need six months" on one side, "I can't save anything anyway without a raise" on the other. Both are true, and neither tells you what to do today.

An emergency fund is not a matter of saving more. It is a matter of **deciding first what the money is meant to survive**.

## Why three months

Three months is not magic. It is the shortest stretch you can hold out when income stops. Finding a new job, recovering from illness, and re-adjusting a large expense generally fit inside that window.

Your own records beat someone else's statistics. How long your last job change took. How many weeks you were completely out of work when you were ill. How long it took for an unexpected cost to become normal again. The longest of those three is your real number. For some people it is two months, for others six.

## Calculate on fixed costs

Not three months of salary. Three months of **fixed costs**.

1. Rent, management fee, loan principal and interest
2. Phone, insurance, subscriptions
3. The minimum food cost
4. The part of transport and medical costs you cannot cut

Add these up for one month of "survival cost," then multiply by three. Leave dining out, leisure and hobbies out of it. An emergency fund is not the money that protects quality of life. It is the money that **stops you from falling over**.

## The order to save in

- **Stage 1: one month** — the minimum that lets you keep your head up when a payslip is delayed or an expense arrives out of nowhere
- **Stage 2: three months** — the line where life does not wobble even if income stops completely
- **Stage 3: beyond that** — from here it is no longer an emergency fund but "funds for choice"

If you start investing before stage one is full, the first time the market wobbles you will pull the emergency fund back out of the market. That is when it is most expensive.

## Three common mistakes with emergency funds

- **Putting it in investments** — the performance of an emergency fund is not yield but immediate withdrawability
- **Aiming for the exact figure** — stopping at 2.7 months instead of three is worse than rounding up to four
- **Using it once and forgetting** — without a refill rule decided in advance, an emergency fund becomes a one-time item

## Records let you refill

When you spend from the fund, leave three lines. How much, why, and when you will refill it. That record turns into a plan that refills automatically from the next month. What matters more than the number is the path back.

Do not take the three-month rule on faith. Instead, write down **what you are trying to survive**. The moment you write it, an emergency fund stops being a measure of fear and becomes a measure of planning.
