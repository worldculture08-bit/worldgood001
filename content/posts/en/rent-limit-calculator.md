"Rent should be under 30% of your monthly income." It is the advice heard most often when preparing to move out. It is not wrong, but if you apply it as-is, most people end up over the limit. Because rent is not the only cost that has to fit inside that 30%.

## Where the 30% rule comes from

It is a rule of thumb: when housing costs exceed 30% of take-home income, spending on food, savings and health tends to get squeezed. The key word is **take-home**. Calculated on gross salary, the real burden is far larger. The starting point is the net pay figure on your payslip, not the annual contract.

## The gap — rent is only part of housing cost

Monthly housing cost is all of the following added together.

- Rent, or the interest on a jeonse loan
- Management fee
- Electricity, gas, water, internet
- One-twelfth of annual costs such as cleaning and repairs at move-out

Deposit loan interest especially must be included: it lowers the monthly rent but replaces it with an interest bill. A 450,000 won rent and 450,000 won of jeonse interest cost the same out of the bank account, yet many people exclude the interest because "that is interest, not rent."

## Three steps

1. **Confirm net pay** — use the after-tax amount on your payslip.
2. **Estimate fixed housing costs other than rent** — for a studio, management fee and utilities run 120,000–180,000 won a month; for an officetel or apartment, 200,000 won or more.
3. **Limit = net pay × 0.3 − fixed housing costs**

## Worked example

Net pay 2.8 million won, management fee 150,000 won, electricity, gas and internet 100,000 won.

- Housing limit = 2.8 million × 0.3 = 840,000 won
- Fixed housing costs = 150,000 + 100,000 = 250,000 won
- Rent limit = 840,000 − 250,000 = **590,000 won**

A 550,000 won room listed on a property site fits inside the limit. But add 120,000 won of deposit loan interest and real housing cost becomes 820,000 won, almost the full 840,000 won limit. Divide one-off costs like moving fees and household purchases across twelve months and the limit drops further.

## Is going over 30% wrong?

Not necessarily. Two cases can justify it.

- **A large cut in commuting time** — priced as money, it may not be a loss. See the hybrid work calculator post for the method.
- **A confirmed income increase** — only count what a contract already fixes, such as an end to a probation period or an approved promotion. "Expected to rise" does not go in the calculation.

Conversely, 30% can still feel tight. If your income is irregular, as a freelancer or on a fixed-term contract, aim for 25% or below. The more volatile the income, the earlier housing cost should be adjusted.

## Verify with records

Track actual housing costs for the three months after moving. The first month bunches up moving and household purchases, so the figures look off. Use the three-month average as the basis:

- Below 85% of the limit — keep it
- Above 95% — recalculate the limit at the next move

Thirty percent is a starting point. The real answer lives in knowing what you actually spent on housing last month.
