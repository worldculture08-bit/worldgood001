A few evenings ago a friend said something like this: "Maybe going to a company will become rare for my children's generation. Then how do they eat?" It came out like a joke, but the table went quiet for a moment.

The end of work did not appear suddenly. The same words were said when work moved from agriculture to factories, and again when it moved from factories to services. Back then jobs shifted and people worked again at the place they shifted to. What is different this time, people say, is that there will be fewer places to shift to.

## The real question is "if work shrinks, how does income grow"

That is the core. Even if work shrinks, people still have to eat. Hence basic income arrives on the table. **Paying a fixed amount to everyone whether they work or not.** Several countries have run experiments, and the results differ. There are reports saying it was not bad, and reports saying the effect was smaller than expected.

In Korea the question that comes up first is about money. "Where does that money come from." That is a fair question. I think the questions in front of basic income matter more than basic income itself. **Who ends up absorbing the work that shrank**, and what happens to the pay of the work that remains.

Honestly, I do not think the "end of work" arrives all at once. It comes slowly, unevenly, by industry and by region. The problem is that people split apart inside that unevenness. Those pushed out first, and those who keep hold of AI. The wider that gap opens, the more unstable society becomes.

That is why the basic income debate keeps returning. It is a conversation about a safety net that says **"you can eat even without work."** Set aside for and against, the fact that this is a conversation about bread rather than about voting rights is what makes it serious.

I think there is one thing that should come before basic income. A society where the people who work are treated with more respect than they are now. Once that holds, the basic income debate gets much easier. A safety net is built not on desperation but on room to breathe.
