Writing from the field is not about selling emotion. It comes down first to **leaving a record of what was there**. That is not a claim that feeling is wrong. It is that when feeling and fact share one sentence, the post becomes something nobody can later believe. Readers usually leave not because a sentence was beautiful, but because the grounds were not visible.

## Three boxes

- **Fact** — date, place, who, what
- **Utterance** — what the other person actually said (in quotes if possible)
- **Interpretation** — what I felt, hypotheses, the next question

Once this distinction becomes a habit, "where does the evidence end" is also clear when you shape the piece. Before publishing, try trimming the interpretation box once more. The post may get drier, but trust goes up. Dry is not the same as dull: when a scene is specific, even a dry post can be vivid.

## Why evidence matters

When a misunderstanding arises in the middle of connecting people and work, the relationship takes damage first. The closer a record is to evidence, the more room there is later for conversation. "I remember him saying this" and "my note says this was written" carry different weight. Especially when several people are involved, comparing records hurts less than competing memories.

When evidence is thin, write honestly: "not confirmed". Writing a guess as fact to fill a blank turns that post into a boomerang later. The virtue of a field record is not completeness but **the possibility of revision**.

Start with today's note and put one line in the fact box, even just that. The more intense the day feels, the more reason to do so. Intense days produce intense distortion.
